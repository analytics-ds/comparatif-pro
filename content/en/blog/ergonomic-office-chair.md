Choosing an **ergonomic office chair** directly affects the comfort, health and productivity of employees who spend most of their day sitting in front of a screen. Between entry-level models sold in general retail and certified professional chairs, the gap in adjustments and durability is considerable. This guide covers the technical criteria to check, price benchmarks and best practices for equipping company workstations, whether in the office or working remotely.

## What is an ergonomic office chair and why adopt it in the workplace

An ergonomic office chair is defined by its ability to adapt to the body and movements of the person using it, rather than the other way around. Unlike a fixed seat, it offers several independent adjustment points: seat height, seat depth, backrest tilt, lumbar support and armrests. This adaptability limits muscle strain built up over long periods of sitting, which make up most of the working day in office-based roles.

For a company, the stakes go beyond individual comfort. A poorly fitted chair fleet increases the risk of sick leave linked to back or neck pain, and weighs on absenteeism and perceived productivity alike. Conversely, investing in adjustable **professional furniture** pays off over several years thanks to better durability and fewer premature replacements.

Some roles are more exposed to this risk than others: development, accounting, customer service or administrative data entry positions often involve seven to eight hours of sitting a day with little change in posture. These are the profiles to prioritise when gradually renewing a chair fleet, before extending the effort to more mobile roles such as reception or field sales.

### The risks of a poorly equipped workstation

A non-adjustable or poorly set chair encourages musculoskeletal disorders (**MSDs**), the leading recognised occupational illness in France. The most common symptoms affect the lower back, neck and shoulders, often worsened by an incorrect screen height or a poorly positioned keyboard alongside the chair. In an open-plan office as much as in an individual office, attention to workstation ergonomics should cover the whole set of chair, desk and screen, not the chair alone.

## The essential technical criteria for choosing an ergonomic chair

Before comparing models, it helps to set a list of non-negotiable criteria. A chair missing any of these adjustments cannot be called fully ergonomic, whatever its marketing position.

### Lumbar support and the seat mechanism

Adjustable **lumbar support**, ideally in both height and depth, is the most decisive criterion against back pain. It should follow the natural curve of the lower back without creating a pressure point. The seat mechanism complements this adjustment: a synchronous mechanism tilts the backrest and seat together at a calculated ratio, following the pelvis's movements without losing contact between the back and the backrest. Basic mechanisms, which only tilt the backrest, offer noticeably less precise support over time.

### Height adjustments, armrests and headrest

Seat height should let the feet rest flat on the floor with thighs parallel to the seat, which requires a wide enough adjustment range to cover most body types (generally 40 to 53 cm from the floor). Armrests adjustable in height, width and sometimes depth relieve the trapezius muscles by preventing the shoulders from hunching up. A headrest, not systematic but recommended beyond 6 to 8 hours of daily use, reduces neck strain during backward tilt.

The choice of seat material also deserves attention. A mesh seat improves ventilation and limits heat build-up during long sessions, at the cost of a sometimes less enveloping support. A high-density foam seat offers more immediate comfort and better lateral support, but requires quality padding to avoid sagging after a few years of intensive use. Neither material is objectively superior, the choice depends on each user's sensitivity to heat and to firmness of support.

## Standard ergonomic chair or premium chair: what are the differences

Models on the market fall into three families, based on the range of adjustments and the quality of materials used. This segmentation helps target the purchase according to the actual use of the workstation: occasional, daily or intensive.

### Comparison table of ergonomic chair ranges

| Range | Indicative price (excl. tax) | Available adjustments | Recommended use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entry-level | 80 to 150 euros | Seat height, basic tilt | Occasional use, less than 4 hours a day |
| Mid-range | 200 to 500 euros | Height, depth, adjustable lumbar support, 3D armrests | Full-time daily workstation |
| Premium | 600 to 1,200 euros and above | Full synchronous mechanism, headrest, sliding seat, 8 to 12-year warranty | Intensive use, sensitive positions or management |

The difference between mid-range and premium mostly comes down to the precision of the adjustments and the durability of the mechanisms, more than a radical ergonomic gain. For a rollout across several workstations, mid-range models cover most of the needs of a standard professional office.

## Budget and price benchmarks for equipping a workstation

The cost of an ergonomic chair is not limited to the purchase price. It should factor in the warranty length, the availability of spare parts (gas lifts, castors, armrests) and delivery costs for bulk orders. A 250-euro chair (excl. tax) with a 5-year warranty is often cheaper over time than a 100-euro model replaced every two years.

The availability of spare parts is a criterion often overlooked at the time of purchase. A gas lift or a set of castors that is no longer available two years after purchase forces the replacement of the entire chair for a minor fault. Manufacturers who guarantee a spare parts stock for the full warranty period limit this risk, a point worth checking explicitly with the supplier before a bulk order.

This budget fits into a broader envelope for equipping the workstation, which also includes the desk and storage units. The guide on [how to choose professional office furniture](/en/blog/choosing-professional-office-furniture/) details price ranges per complete workstation and the standards applicable to office furniture as a whole, including the EN 1335 standard specific to office chairs.

## Ergonomic chairs and remote work: equipping remote workstations

The rise of **remote work** has shifted part of the ergonomic challenge outside the company's premises. An employee who works two or more days a week from home deserves the same level of equipment as in the office, otherwise the benefits of a professional ergonomic chair at work are cancelled out by a makeshift seat at home.

Several companies now include an ergonomic chair in their remote work equipment policy, delivered directly to the employee's home. Compact or foldable models exist for smaller spaces, but they frequently sacrifice adjustable lumbar support: this criterion should remain non-negotiable even in a reduced format.

Two approaches coexist for organising this remote equipment: providing a single model chosen by the employer, simpler to manage and negotiate in bulk, or a budget allowance left to the employee's choice among a list of models validated by facilities management. The second option makes adoption easier, provided a minimum set of technical criteria (minimum adjustments, adjustable lumbar support) is set so that a low price doesn't take priority over the actual ergonomics of the chosen chair.

## Adjusting your ergonomic chair correctly day to day

A technically complete chair provides no benefit if left poorly adjusted. Most users settle for the factory setting, whereas each parameter should be adjusted individually.

### Recommended adjustment steps

1. Set the seat height so the feet rest flat on the floor, knees at a right angle.
2. Adjust the seat depth to leave two to three fingers of space between the front edge of the seat and the back of the knees.
3. Position the lumbar support at the level of the natural curve of the lower back.
4. Adjust the backrest tilt and activate the synchronous mechanism for a slight recline during listening or reading tasks.
5. Adjust the armrests to elbow height, arms relaxed, to relieve the trapezius muscles.

This initial setting should be revisited after a few days of use, once the first feelings of discomfort or comfort have been identified.

## Bulk purchasing and equipment policy: best practices for facilities teams

For a facilities management or human resources department equipping several dozen workstations, the challenge shifts from selecting a single model to defining a coherent purchasing policy. Referencing two or three models by role profile (sedentary, mobile, management) simplifies fleet management and price negotiations compared with leaving the choice to each individual employee.

This equipment policy usually ties in with a broader fit-out project, particularly during a move to an open-plan layout or a reorganisation of work areas. The article on [how to design an open-plan office](/en/blog/how-to-design-open-plan-office/) covers the acoustic and functional criteria to factor in alongside the choice of seating. Planning a renewal budget over five to eight years, aligned with the average warranty length of mid-range and premium models, limits scattered purchases and comfort gaps between teams.

Before placing a bulk order, testing two or three shortlisted models for one to two weeks with a small panel of volunteer employees remains the most reliable way to validate a choice. Real-world feedback, particularly on lumbar support comfort after several consecutive days, usefully complements supplier data sheets, which rarely describe differences in feel between body types.
